  8. 2.1The recovery timeline

    This section now combines the old 1.5 (growth stages) and 2.1 (recovery timeline). The lookup tool screenshot still shows growth stages as section 1.5 on the Part 1 tab; retake it once the tool change is on GitHub.

    The mammal paragraph is new and draws on Hale and others (2022, 2016). The “What you see” table now uses the four stage names of the first table rather than its own five; worth an ecologist’s check of both.

    The photo sequence above is from the example block. Its caption says the December 2024 fire, but the state record and the project’s site records give the 2025 Yarram Gap Road fire (January 2025). Confirm the date with the landholder.

    The Inland Plains Woodland, Treed Swampy Wetland and Basalt Grassland rows fold Cheal’s five stages for each into the guide’s four (renewal and juvenility together as Juvenile, the last stage as Old). Worth an ecologist’s check.

    Which term throughout — “young” or “adolescent”? Cheal’s formal stages for Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest run renewal, juvenility, adolescence, maturity, waning, senescence; Forby Forest has vigorous maturity and stasis instead of maturity and waning, so the two divisions do not even share a vocabulary. Recommendation: keep the plain set in the table and gloss the formal terms once in the glossary. The percentages above are attributed to La Trobe University work — that publication still needs pinning down.

  9. 2.2How plants come back — and which ones won’t

    The rules of thumb above need a botanist’s check — they are broadly right at family level but there are exceptions in every genus.     The lookup tool shows Silver Banksia as Usually killed by fire (the published records disagree), while this section and Pauline Rudolph’s talk say it does both. Worth checking which is right for this district, and whether the tool needs a local override.

    The plant figures for the example block (120 plants, 95 resprouters, 23 seeders; nine under Survey for these first and ten fine until 2030 on the Heathy Woodland tab) were read from the live lookup tool on 2 October 2026. The groups change as the years pass, so recheck against the screenshot before publishing. The tool also gives two different hakeas the common name Bushy Needlewood; one of them needs correcting in its data.

    Re-run the one-kilometre Atlas of Living Australia search (in A broader list) for the new example block, and add the species count back if it is useful.
