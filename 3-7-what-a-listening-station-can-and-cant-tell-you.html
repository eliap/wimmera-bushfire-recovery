3.7

What a listening station can and can’t tell you

Three things are worth knowing before you buy one, and none of them are in the manual.

1. It hears its own patch, not your property

We tested this against an experienced observer surveying a plot while we checked what the nearest station logged in the same twenty minutes. Standing in the plot, the station picked up roughly one of the birds the observer recorded. Several hundred metres away it picked up essentially none of them, and logged a different set of birds instead.

So a station’s list belongs to the ground it stands on. One unit does not cover a property, and if there is unburnt bush within earshot, some of what it hears is coming from there.

2. It will be offline more than you expect

Across our nine stations the network ran less than half the time, and by late winter seven of the nine were down — flat batteries, dropped coverage, water where it shouldn’t be. Check yours weekly. And read a quiet month as a flat battery before you read it as quiet bush.

3. It will name the odd wrong bird

Identification is done by an AI model listening for call patterns. It is right most of the time. One of our stations, beside a farm dam a long way inland, confidently reported a seabird. Treat an unusual record as a question — the recording is saved, so you or a local birder can check it.

What it is good at, and what it can’t replace

A station listens every day, at dawn, dusk and two in the morning, for months. Over the year ours picked up 117 species across the nine properties; expert observers surveying the same properties seasonally recorded 59. Night-callers, cryptic species and the bird that turns up once in June are the station’s territory.

But some birds barely vocalise. Our observers recorded a goshawk, a kite, a falcon and a firetail that no station ever picked up, and one parrot recorded by observers at five properties was never detected acoustically. Do both if you can. If you can only do one, the station is the one you can afford to run forever.

What we found

Not a substitute for an expert survey, but the most practical way we found for a landholder to keep track of their own recovery.1
