2.2

How plants come back — and which ones won’t

In the first weeks the bush looks dead. Most of it isn’t. Nearly every plant on your block uses one of four strategies, and knowing which is which tells you what to expect — and which species to worry about.

1. Resprouters — back in weeks

Growing points protected under thick bark or underground. Eucalypts shoot straight down the trunk and along the limbs; grasses and bracken come from underground; grass trees have been recorded shooting within a fortnight.12

Look for: shoots emerging from bark, fresh growth at the base of a trunk, green spears in a burnt tussock. If you see this, the plant is fine.

2. Obligate seeders — killed, and starting over

Killed outright, returning only from seed in the soil or released from woody fruit by heat. Most local banksias, hakeas, wattles and peas.342

Look for: nothing at first, then a carpet of seedlings, usually late in the first year. The parent plants stay dead — cutting them back achieves nothing.

3. Fire-cued germinators — things you have never seen

Seed that sits in the soil for decades until smoke or heat breaks dormancy. It is why people find orchids, lilies and peas they are certain were never there — the orchid response after Black Saturday was documented in detail for exactly this reason.5 After the 2006 Mt Lubra fire, monitoring turned up entirely new populations of threatened plants in country unburnt for fifty or sixty years.4

Look for: anything unfamiliar, especially in the first spring. Much of it won’t be visible again for decades.

4. The space-takers

Plants built to exploit bare ground, full sun and no competition. Among natives these are the fire ephemerals: fast, short-lived, and useful for holding soil and feeding insects and seed-eaters.2 The same opening is what weeds are built for — see Part 4.

Your plant list in the lookup tool

In the lookup tool: Plants that belong in your bush

On the Part 2 tab, the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool lists the native plants recorded in survey plots of your vegetation type across your bioregion, with how each one handles fire. There is a tab for each vegetation type on your block, plus an All together tab that combines them into one list for the whole block. The vegetation you are looking at shows in yellow on the map. Seeders that need watching are pulled out to the top.

Each plant card shows:

  • How common it is — the bar and percentage are the share of survey plots of this vegetation type where the plant was recorded. The list is in that order, most common first.
  • How it handles fire — Resprouts or Killed by fire, regrows from seed. Where the published records disagree, it says Usually.
  • Where its seed is kept — in the soil, or held on the plant in woody fruit until fire releases it.
  • Seeds at … yrs — how many years a seedling takes to set its first seed.
  • Too young to seed since … — for seeders, a warning that not enough years have passed since this vegetation last burnt on your block for new plants to have set seed. May be seeding again since … means it is borderline.

Above the full list, the seeders that need attention are pulled into two groups:

  • Survey for these first — shown where part of your block burnt twice within the time these plants need to set seed, so they may have had no seed on board when the second fire came. Plants that hold their seed in woody fruit, like banksias and hakeas, are most at risk, because the second fire burns the seed before it drops.
  • Should be fine if fire stays out until … — the other seeders, still growing back from the last fire. The year is when the slowest of them should be seeding, but only if the seedlings get the chance to grow: weeds that smother them, or rabbits, deer and other grazers that eat them, can wipe them out just as surely as a fire that comes too soon (Part 4).

Use the filters to show one plant type (shrubs, grasses, herbs) or one fire response. Tap a plant’s name to see it on iNaturalist.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

On the Heathy Woodland tab, the lookup tool lists 120 native plants that turn up regularly in 227 survey plots of Heathy Woodland in the Greater Grampians, surveyed between 1976 and 2021. Of those with fire data, 95 resprout and 23 are killed by fire and come back from seed. The most common are Heath Tea-tree (in 92% of plots), Flame Heath, Austral Grass-tree, Tassel Rope-rush and Common Beard-heath. All of them resprout, which is why this country greens up quickly after a fire.

The seeders are the watch list, and on this block the lookup tool has already sorted them. Because 67 hectares burnt in 2006 and again in 2025, only 19 years apart, it puts nine seeders under Survey for these first, among them Silver Banksia, Showy Parrot-pea, Cat’s Claw Grevillea, Desert Banksia, two needlewood hakeas and Golden Wattle. They take five to twenty years to set their first seed, so some may have had none on board when the 2025 fire came. The banksias and hakeas hold their seed on the plant, which makes them the most exposed.

The other ten seeders sit under Should be fine if fire stays out until 2030, led by Common Fringe-myrtle and Pine Heath. They are growing back from the 2025 fire and should all be seeding by then, as long as weeds and grazing animals don’t get to the seedlings first.

The Shrubby Woodland and Damp Sands Herb-rich Woodland tabs are each built from far fewer plots (41), so read them more loosely. Shrubby Woodland shows the same two groups. The Damp Sands Herb-rich Woodland escaped the 2025 fire, so none of its seeders are flagged.

What we would watch here: the seeders on the ground that burnt twice. If the parrot-peas, banksias and wattles are thin there in the third year while the stringybarks and grass-trees look fine, the canopy has recovered and the shrub layer has not. That is the pattern to catch early (section 3.3), because it changes what the block can support for decades. The ground that escaped in 2025 is the comparison: it shows what the shrub layer should look like.

Sorting your own list

This is worth doing as an exercise rather than just reading. Start from the lookup tool’s list, or the list you have been building on iNaturalist (6.1), and put every species you find on your block into one of the four groups.

Protocol — sorting your species by fire response
Start with the lookup tool
The lookup tool’s plant list already sorts the plants of your vegetation type into resprouters and seeders, from published fire-trait records (mainly DEECA’s Vital Attributes database). It won’t hold every plant on your block, but it will place most of them.
Use the rules of thumb for the rest
Resprouters: eucalypts, grass-trees, grasses and sedges, mat-rushes, bracken. Seeders: most peas, banksias, hakeas, grevilleas and tea-trees.26
Let the fire settle it
After a fire the plant tells you. Reshooting from bark or base, it is a resprouter. Coming back only as seedlings while the parents stay dead, it is a seeder. This is the most reliable sorting you will get, and it needs no expertise at all. It is also, near enough, the method the department uses: walk an area where you know the time since fire, and record for each species whether it came back from seed or resprouted.7
Ask about the ones you can’t place
Some species do both — resprouting where the fire was mild, reseeding where it was hot. Silver Banksia, the most familiar shrub in much of the district, is one of them.4 Your Landcare group or a local ecologist will sort those out.
Mark the seeders
That list is your watch list — in effect your block’s version of the key fire-response species that set the intervals in 1.4. Everything in the rest of this section is about them. The lookup tool’s Survey for these first group and Too young to seed flags tell you which are most exposed right now.

Why the seeders are the watch list

A resprouter has to be killed outright to be lost, which takes an extraordinary fire. A seeder only has to be caught at the wrong moment.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A seedling must survive, grow, flower, then keep flowering for several more seasons before there is a seed store worth anything in the ground. After the Mt Lubra fire, Grampians Rice-flower took two years to flower for the first time and Grampians Bitter-pea three — and flowering is the start, not the end.4

A mass germination is no guarantee either. Monitoring of the Mt Cassell Grevillea counted 294 seedlings in 2024 and 214 a year later — 73% survival.4 A reasonable result, and a reminder that the seedlings you see now are not the plants you will have in five years.

Burn that ground again before the seeders have rebuilt their seedbank and they are gone from the patch. That is the minimum interval in 1.4, in practice — and it is why comparing your fire history against your seeder list is the single most useful thing you can do with both. On the example block, the gap between the last two fires was 19 years. Grampians Rice-flower, which has records in the surrounding country, needed two of those to reach first flower and several more to build a seedbank worth anything. It made it — but a fire in, say, 2015 would have caught it partway.

What to watch for

  • A shrub layer that stays thin. An area that used to carry dense shrubs still open in year two or three, while the rest fills in. Compare it against less severely burnt ground.
  • A species on your seeder list you cannot find. Particularly if your place has burnt twice in twenty years.
  • Seedlings that appear then vanish. Browsed-off germination is the same outcome as no germination. After a fire there is little else for deer, rabbits and kangaroos to eat (4.4).
A broader list

The lookup tool’s list is the plants of your vegetation type. For everything recorded in the country around you, search your area on the Atlas of Living Australia, draw a radius around your block, and download the species checklist. Be clear about what it is: species recorded near you, not growing on you — and only what has been recorded, which in lightly surveyed country can be very little. Because research-grade iNaturalist records flow into the Atlas, what you record walking your block becomes part of what the next person’s search finds.

Worth holding onto

A severe fire changes the state of a place, sometimes dramatically, but that change is not usually permanent. This country evolved with fire and given a run without a second burn will rebuild itself. Most of the work in this guide is about removing what gets in the way, not replacing what was lost.

Needed

The rules of thumb above need a botanist’s check — they are broadly right at family level but there are exceptions in every genus. The lookup tool’s fire-response data comes from AusTraits,8 mainly DEECA’s Vital Attributes, which could be a first cross-check before it goes to Huib or a local ecologist.

The lookup tool shows Silver Banksia as Usually killed by fire (the published records disagree), while this section and Pauline Rudolph’s talk say it does both. Worth checking which is right for this district, and whether the tool needs a local override.

The plant figures for the example block (120 plants, 95 resprouters, 23 seeders; nine under Survey for these first and ten fine until 2030 on the Heathy Woodland tab) were read from the live lookup tool on 2 October 2026. The groups change as the years pass, so recheck against the screenshot before publishing. The tool also gives two different hakeas the common name Bushy Needlewood; one of them needs correcting in its data.

Re-run the one-kilometre Atlas of Living Australia search (in A broader list) for the new example block, and add the species count back if it is useful.
