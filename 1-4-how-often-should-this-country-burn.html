1.4

How often should this country burn?

The question is not whether fire is good or bad for the bush, but whether this fire, at this severity, this long after the last one, is something your vegetation can absorb. Victorian fire ecologists answer it with two numbers per vegetation type: a minimum and a maximum tolerable fire interval.1

The minimum

The shortest gap between fires a community can take without losing species. It is set by the plants fire kills outright, which return only from seed — obligate seeders, including most local peas, wattles, banksias and hakeas. A seeder must grow, flower, and build a seed store before the next fire. If fire returns first, there is no seed left and the species is gone from that patch.1

The timeframes are long. After the 2006 Mt Lubra fire, Grampians Rice-flower took two years to first flower and Grampians Bitter-pea three — and flowering is the start, not the end.2

There are two minimums, because severity matters as much as timing:1

  • After a high-severity fire — crowns burnt, few unburnt patches — the minimum is longer. Nothing nearby is left to reseed from.
  • After a low-severity, patchy fire — canopy largely intact, plenty of unburnt islands — it is shorter. The unburnt patches carry the community through — patchiness is what lets sensitive species survive a short interval, because some of them escape being burnt at all.1

Which is why an unburnt patch in the middle of your block is worth protecting well out of proportion to its size.

The maximum

The longest a community can go without fire before it declines.1 Grasslands choke on their own thatch and lose their herbs. Some species won’t germinate until smoke or heat triggers them. A long absence of fire is not automatically a good outcome.

Where the numbers come from

The intervals are not a general judgement about fire. A vegetation type contains a hundred or more plant species, and within that, usually ten to fifteen are the sensitive ones — some to being burnt too often, some to going too long without it. Those species, the key fire-response species, are what set the minimum and the maximum. Everything else in the community can tolerate a wider range.31

So the table is really a statement about particular plants. If you know which of them grow on your block (2.2), the numbers stop being abstract and become a list of things to watch for.

Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest is the most widespread type in the Grampians foothills. It recovers fast and much of its regeneration is fire-cued — but burnt severely twice inside fifteen years, its seeders start dropping out. Country that burnt in 2006 and again in 2024 or 2025 sits on that edge.

In the lookup tool: section 1.4

In the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool, section 1.4, How often should this country burn?, does the reading across for you. It opens with a one-line verdict, then the rows of this table that apply to your block, then a timeline for each vegetation division on the block, laid against its minimum and maximum.

Reading the timelines

  • Dots are fire years. The bar between two dots is the gap between those fires, labelled in years.
  • Green means the gap was long enough. Yellow means it was long enough only if the earlier fire was patchy. Red means the gap fell inside the minimum.
  • The hatched stretch after the last fire is the time the seeders need to rebuild. The red line at its end is the first year another fire would clear the minimum. A line marked max shows when the division reaches its maximum.
  • Under each timeline, a line for each gap (OK, depends on severity, inside minimum) and one for where the bush is now: recovering (another fire would come too soon), within range, or past maximum.

The severity you set in section 1.3 changes the answer. Where it isn’t known, the lookup tool says so and gives the answer both ways.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

The verdict for the example block: “Your bush is recovering from its last fire and within its tolerable range. Another fire before 2039 would come too soon.”

Heathy Woodland (Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest). Fires in 1939, 2006 and 2024. Both gaps, 67 years and 18 years, are marked OK against the 10–15 year minimum. At 18 years, the 2006-to-2024 gap clears the minimum, but not by much. Because the severity of the 2024 fire isn’t settled in the record, the lookup tool gives two dates: another fire before 2039 would be too soon after a high-severity fire, or before 2034 if the 2024 fire was patchy here.

Damp Sands Herb-rich Woodland (Forby Forest). The same three fires. Forby Forest has a 15-year minimum whatever the severity, so the date is 2039 either way.

One thing the lookup tool doesn’t flag: it checks each gap only against the minimum. The 67-year gap before 2006 was longer than the 45-year maximum for Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest, which is its own kind of signal. Check the maximum column of the table yourself.

What the numbers don’t tell you

  • They describe a whole vegetation class; your patch may sit at one end of it.
  • Mapped fire boundaries are perimeters, not severity.
  • They say nothing about drought, grazing or weeds, which can matter more in a given year.
  • Vegetation types outside these five divisions (much of the Little Desert, for now) are listed in the lookup tool but not assessed. Where the record shows no fire at all, the lookup tool counts from 1939, and a blank record is not proof that nothing burnt (section 1.3).

Use the numbers to know whether to relax or pay attention. What you see on the ground over the next few seasons (Part 2) tells you more.

Across the nine demonstration properties, most blocks had carried at most two fires in twenty years and the regeneration flush has been strong.4
