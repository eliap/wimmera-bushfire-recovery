1.1

Bioregions and geology

To get to know your block, there are a few things you can look up that tell you what sort of habitat it has, and how that habitat responds to fire.

Fire ecology is shaped by vegetation type, which is shaped by climate and by soil, which in turn is shaped by geology. So the first step is to find your overall climate, soil and geology type: your bioregion.

What is a bioregion?

A bioregion is a big stretch of country that has broadly similar landforms, rocks, soils and climate, so it grows a recognisable mix of plants and supports a recognisable mix of animals.

Victoria is divided into 28 bioregions. They are drawn at a landscape scale, tens to hundreds of kilometres across, so your bioregion tells you what kind of country you are in, not exactly what grows on your block. That finer detail is your vegetation type (section 1.2). The bioregion is the big picture it sits inside.

This guide covers four bioregions, the ones that cover the fire-affected country of the Wimmera and Grampians.1 If yours is one of them, the lookup tool shows its landform and the vegetation you’d expect from this table.

What it means for fire

  • Sandy, infertile, well-drained soils grow heathy vegetation: flammable most of the year, burns hot, depends on fire to regenerate.2
  • Basalt plains grow grassland, which carries fast, light fires and needs them fairly often to stay diverse.
  • Swampy ground rarely carries fire and can act as a firebreak for what adjoins it.

Bioregion and geology are both mapped at a landscape scale, so the vegetation type mapped on your own ground (section 1.2) is the more precise answer.

In the lookup tool: section 1.1

In the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool, look at section 1.1, Bioregion and geology. It tells you your bioregion and geology, and what that generally means for fire.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

The example block sits in the Greater Grampians bioregion: uplifted, faulted sedimentary country on the edge of the ranges, with the shallow, well-drained soils that go with it. The geology table puts almost all of the block on colluvium: gravel, sand and silt washed down the gullies from the ranges in recent geological time. A sliver, under 1%, sits on the Grampians Group sandstone that the ranges themselves are made of. Sandy, well-drained ground like that grows heathy vegetation, which is why the lookup tool’s “What it means for fire” note says it is flammable most of the year, burns hot, and depends on fire to regenerate.
