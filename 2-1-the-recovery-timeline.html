2.1

The recovery timeline

Vegetation growth after a fire progresses in recognisable stages. Fire ecologists call these growth stages, and count them from the last fire. Two patches of the same vegetation type, both in good condition, can look nothing alike, and the usual reason is how long since each last burnt. No stage is the “good” one. Different plants and animals prefer different growth stages, so the highest biodiversity generally comes with a mosaic of growth stages.

How long each stage lasts

How long each stage lasts depends on the vegetation, so the stages are set for each fire division (section 1.2).

Years since fire for each growth stage, in the region’s common vegetation divisions.1
Vegetation divisionJuvenileYoungMatureOld
Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest0–33–1010–3535+
Forby Forest0–33–1010–150150+
Inland Plains Woodland0–44–1919–120120+
Treed Swampy Wetland0–33–88–3030+
Basalt Grassland0–22–44–66+

Note how different the rows are. Bush that burnt thirty-five years ago is old in Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest and barely middle-aged in Forby Forest, while Basalt Grassland is old just six years after a fire.

Inland Plains Woodland rarely carries fire at all. Its stages describe what follows the unusual fire that does get through, and its 150-year maximum is only a best estimate. Some stands can be degraded by a single fire.

What you should see at each stage

What to expect at each growth stage. When each stage starts and ends depends on your fire division (table above), and on how severe the fire was and the season it came in.1
StageWhat you see
JuvenileIn the first months, black ground and then a green fuzz. Resprouters shoot, grass trees within a fortnight.2 Surviving animals are suddenly very visible.3 Then fire ephemerals carpet the bare ground and hold the soil, seedlings of the plants the fire killed appear, and bird diversity is still low.
YoungThe shrub layer closes in. Small mammals are well down but climbing; some peak around ten years after fire.43
MatureThe second generation of plants killed by the fire reaches full size and sets seed properly. Logs and litter build up, and the bush gets more varied in structure.
OldMammal populations have recovered, and this is where most hollows are. In country that relies on fire to regenerate, it needs fire again.
In the lookup tool: section 2.1

On the Part 2 tab of the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool, section 2.1, Growth stages, works out the stage of each part of your block from the years since it last burnt, using the first table above. While the section is on screen, the map colours each part of the block by its stage, from pale green for juvenile to dark green for old. Click a row to highlight that part of the block.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

The 67 hectares of Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest that burnt in the 2025 Yarram Gap Road fire is juvenile, early in a stage that runs to three years. By its second spring, in late 2026, fire ephemerals should be carpeting the bare ground there and seedlings of the plants the fire killed should be showing. The 22 hectares that escaped last burnt in 2006, twenty years ago, so it is mature (10–35 years in its division), and the Damp Sands Herb-rich Woodland is early in Forby Forest’s long mature stage. The block now carries two stages side by side, and the older one is the scarcer: about four-fifths of the bush within 5 km burnt in 2025 (section 1.3), so the block’s unburnt bush is one of the patches the surrounding country will recover from.

Wildlife in the first few years

In the first six months you may see more wildlife than usual. That is exposure, not recovery — the survivors have nowhere to hide. Numbers usually fall from here as food and shelter run out and predators find them easy — predation pressure measurably rises once cover is gone. This is when pest control does the most good.567

Small mammals mostly come back from animals that survived the fire on the spot, in unburnt patches, logs, rock piles and burrows, rather than from animals moving in from outside.3 That is one more reason the unburnt patches on your block matter. Expect numbers to stay well down for the first few years and climb as the shrub layer thickens; some species peak around ten years after fire, and drought can hold the climb back.4

After a high-severity fire, bird diversity can stay low for three years or more, so a quiet block in the second year is normal — and in the Grampians heathy woodlands, patchy burns held more species than wildfire did.8

The biggest disruptions to healthy post fire renewal

Drought first, then browsing pressure, then weeds. Two of the three are things you can act on.79

Why a mix matters

Different animals need different stages at the same time. Young regrowth is dense at ground level, good for small mammals and low-feeding birds. Mature bush has structural variety and accumulated logs. Old bush is where most hollows are. Old vegetation is now managed as an asset in its own right on public land, precisely because it is so hard to replace.7

The state’s benchmarks for public land give a sense of balance: roughly 5% juvenile, 10% adolescent, 30% mature and 20% old, with no more than 30% of fire-sensitive types below their minimum interval. These are landscape-wide targets, not property targets.710

What it means for your block

If everything around you burnt and a corner of yours didn’t, that corner may hold the only deep litter, dense shelter and standing midstorey for some distance, and may be carrying animals with nowhere else to go. In the lookup tool it shows up in section 2.1 as mature or old ground beside juvenile, as on the example block, and Your block in the landscape at the end of section 1.3 shows how much unburnt bush there is around you. When fires have come back before the minimum tolerable interval was reached, protecting the unburnt areas you already have is generally the most valuable thing you can do.

The reverse also holds: surrounded by long-unburnt country, your young regrowth may be the scarce stage, and protecting the rare species that pop up after the fire from weeds and grazing may be the most valuable thing you can do.

A note

Growth stage is counted from the last fire, which, as we discussed in 1.3, may not show unburnt patches within the fire boundary. So a patch can read as juvenile while still holding some old trees that survived. A burnt paddock tree with hollows intact is old-growth habitat whatever the age class says. Mapping your good hollow trees is a very helpful activity, covered later in the guide (section 4.5).
