Front

Before you start

This guide is for anyone with native bush on their place. It is designed in particular for someone who has been recently affected by fire, to help you navigate what comes next for native habitat. But it also is designed for anyone who wants to take preparatory steps to be ready to support your native bush through any future fires.

This guide comes out of a year of fire recovery work done in collaboration with landholders of nine properties burnt by the 2024 Pomonal–Dadswells Bridge fire and the 2025 Grampians and Little Desert fires.

This preamble section explains how to use the guide, where the advice comes from, and why a single block matters as a whole landscape recovers from fire.

0.1

Start here

If fire has just been through your place, we’re sorry. Losing bush you’ve known for years, whether you planted it or spent years walking through it, is a real loss. We know your first priorities will be the urgent things like fences to fix, stock to sort out and insurance forms to fill in. But we also know that many landholders care deeply about the wildlife and plants that share their place, and want to know how to help them recover.

Most of the bush in this part of Victoria has been burning and coming back for a very long time, and much of what looks dead right now is already getting ready to regrow. To know where our interventions can be most helpful, we need to understand what a natural fire recovery looks like in our highly fire-adapted landscape, and where this natural recovery can get off track.

That is what this guide aims to do: help you identify where you can most help your bush recover.

The five questions

The guide is built around the most common questions people have asked us after the 2024 and 2025 bushfires:

  1. Was this a ‘good fire’ or a ‘bad fire’ for my block? What sort of country is this, and how does it respond to fire? (Part 1)
  2. What happens next? What should recover, in what order, and how long will it take? (Part 2)
  3. Is my block recovering the way it should? How to track what survived and what is coming back. (Part 3)
  4. What should I actually do? Weeds, pests, hollows, thinning, replanting — and in what order. (Part 4)
  5. So what do I do first? (Part 5)
If your bush hasn’t burnt

This guide is also designed for folks who are caring for bushland that has not been recently burnt. Just like the fire plan you have developed ahead of time for your family and house, you can prepare a biodiversity-focused fire plan before a fire occurs. To learn how to collect information that will help you make the best decisions possible after a future fire, start at Part 6. It is short, and everything in it is worth more done now than after the event.

What this guide doesn’t cover

This is about native bush on your place, not the garden around your house. For defendable space, plant flammability, firewise garden design and what to put near the buildings, see resources such as the CFA’s Landscaping for Bushfire guide or the Pyrenees Firewise Garden Guide, which Project Platypus wrote with Pyrenees Shire Council in 2026.

We also don’t cover:

  • Fire management — planned burning, permits, fuel reduction. Talk to your local CFA for information about planned burns on your property.
  • Hazard trees and what you are permitted to clear. Burnt trees near buildings, tracks and fencelines are a safety and regulatory question rather than a habitat one, and the rules vary with your zoning. Ask your council or a local arborist. We do ask you to keep standing dead trees where they can safely be kept, to preserve habitat (4.5), but safety comes first.
  • Agricultural recovery — fencing, stock, pasture and soils. Talk to your local CMA or Agriculture Victoria.

0.2

How this guide was made

After bushfires affected private bushland along with national parks in the summers of 2024 and 2025, three of our Wimmera Landcare networks were funded to develop a program to assist folks caring for bushfire-affected native habitat on private property.

The Wimmera Landcare Fire Recovery Partnership Project involved selecting nine demonstration properties across the three Landcare regions, burnt by the 2024 Pomonal–Dadswells Bridge fire and the 2025 Grampians and Little Desert fires. Over 2026, we worked with the landholders to try out simple methods for answering those big questions after fire, and to take on-ground steps to help the bush recover.

We tried out several different ways to monitor the return of biodiversity after fire on these properties. This included automated bird listening stations and trail cameras, seasonal surveys with local bird experts, thermal-scope night surveys, simple counts of hollow-bearing tree density, recording native plant species as they returned, and mapping weeds. We then used those results to help each landholder get started with on-ground interventions, including nest boxes and weed control.

Everything we learned from those nine properties has been compiled into this guide, designed to support anyone who cares for fire-affected native bush on their property.

The project was led by Project Platypus with Wimmera Catchment Management Authority, Horsham District Landcare Network and Hindmarsh Landcare, and was jointly funded by the Commonwealth and Victoria under the Disaster Recovery Funding Arrangements through DEECA’s Grampians Recovery Package.

This project was funded as the Victorian Government recognised that private landholders also play an important role in protecting biodiversity on private land after fire, just as Parks Victoria looks after the park. Your block, however small, is part of how this landscape recovers.

Thank you to our nine demonstration site landholders, to local Landcare groups, and to the Stawell and Pomonal Men’s Sheds.

0.3

Why your block matters

We live in fire-adapted country. The bush around Gariwerd and the Little Desert has been burning and coming back for a very long time, and many of its plants and animals rely on fire to renew. So why do ecologists get so worried when big fires come through?

The answer lies in the concept of habitat loss and fragmentation. Before 1750, native vegetation covered this whole region: grasslands, grassy and heathy woodlands, forests and wetlands, all joined up. Today most of it has been cleared, and what remains sits mainly in a few big national parks, such as Gariwerd, the Little Desert and the Pyrenees, with very little bush left in between. The parks have become islands.

Then, in the space of two years, fire burnt around 70% of the Grampians National Park and nearly all of the Little Desert. In a joined-up landscape, animals could move into unburnt country next door and come back later. In a fragmented one, there often isn’t anywhere to go.

Much of the land around the parks, near fire edges, is privately owned: remnant habitat on bush blocks, and old habitat trees along roadsides and in paddocks. It could be that your block was only partially affected by fire and still has some intact mature remnant bushland; that country is the refuge wildlife moves into while the parks recover. Or even if your block burnt completely, it may be nearer to bush that survived, or it may have burnt less fiercely than the park thanks to the efforts of firefighters. This means your block may be right on the interface where animals and plants start moving back into the burnt country. What you do there can help that return.

Part 1

Where you are

After a fire, most people want to know one thing: was this a “good fire” or a “bad fire” for my block?

A fire can’t be classified quite so easily, but we can get a sense of how challenging recovery might be for sensitive species by learning how that country has adapted to burn. Part 1 will help you work out what sort of country you have: your bioregion and geology, your vegetation type, and when your place burnt before this.

This will tell you how resilient your block likely is to fire and whether it was at a stage where its plants and animals were ready for it. This can help you work out whether any species will need extra help as the habitat renews.

Start by looking up your block

Open the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool, built as part of the Wimmera Landcare Fire Recovery Partnership. It brings together publicly available state data on your block’s bioregion and geology, vegetation type, fire history, fire intervals and growth stage, and lists the plants that belong in your bush.

1.1

Bioregions and geology

To get to know your block, there are a few things you can look up that tell you what sort of habitat it has, and how that habitat responds to fire.

Fire ecology is shaped by vegetation type, which is shaped by climate and by soil, which in turn is shaped by geology. So the first step is to find your overall climate, soil and geology type: your bioregion.

What is a bioregion?

A bioregion is a big stretch of country that has broadly similar landforms, rocks, soils and climate, so it grows a recognisable mix of plants and supports a recognisable mix of animals.

Victoria is divided into 28 bioregions. They are drawn at a landscape scale, tens to hundreds of kilometres across, so your bioregion tells you what kind of country you are in, not exactly what grows on your block. That finer detail is your vegetation type (section 1.2). The bioregion is the big picture it sits inside.

This guide covers five bioregions: the four that cover the fire-affected country of the Wimmera and Grampians,1 and the Lowan Mallee of the Little Desert.27

1.2

Finding your vegetation type

How often your bush should burn, what should return on its own, and what plants are appropriate to plant there all depend on which vegetation type you have.

Every bioregion has a list of possible vegetation types. A vegetation type is a particular community of plants that grows wherever the same conditions repeat, such as the same soil, slope, drainage and rainfall. Each one has its own recognisable mix of trees, shrubs, grasses and herbs. A single block can have several vegetation types side by side, set by changes in the landscape, such as heathy woodland on a sandy rise and herb-rich woodland down in a damp gully.

Victoria’s official name for these vegetation types is Ecological Vegetation Class, or EVC. The state has sorted all its native bush into about 300 of these classes, each with a name and a number, such as Heathy Woodland (48) or Plains Grassy Woodland (55). It has mapped them across Victoria, both as the bush stands today and as it was before 1750. The conservation status listed for each EVC is based on how much of it has been lost since 1750.

Once you know your EVC, you can find out how it responds to fire. The state has sorted every EVC into a fire division: a group of EVCs that behave in much the same way when fire comes through. Victoria’s 300 or so EVCs fall into 32 of these divisions.2 Within one division, the bush recovers at about the same pace and has the same sensitive plants to protect. So instead of setting fire rules for every vegetation type, ecologists set them once for each division: how soon after one fire the next can safely come, how long the bush can go without fire, and how long it takes to grow from young regrowth to old bush.

With that knowledge, you can look up your own EVCs and fire divisions.

1.3

Your property’s fire history

At this stage we know our block’s vegetation types and how they are expected to respond to fire.

So now we need the block’s fire history. How the bush responds to the most recent fire depends on how often it has burnt before. We are interested in the interval between fires, not just any single fire.

The lookup tool uses the Victorian Government’s fire history maps, which go back to 1939. Before you look up your fire history, there are a few things to keep in mind.

Not all fires have been mapped. Coverage is best for large fires on public land. Small and older fires on freehold are recorded patchily. A blank is not evidence that nothing burnt.

The map shows perimeters, not severity. The fire history maps don’t show the difference between an extremely hot fire that removed entire canopy layers and a cooler, patchier fire. Ground shown inside a fire boundary may still contain patches that didn’t burn. The state has separate severity mapping for some recent fires, modelled from aerial and satellite imagery, and the lookup tool uses it where it exists.34

Your block in the landscape

Your block’s fire history means more once you know what happened to the bush around it. A block that escaped when most of the country around it burnt is a refuge: it may be sheltering animals with nowhere else to go, and it holds the seed and shelter the burnt country will recover from. A block that burnt when the bush around it didn’t is a burnt island: plants and animals have unburnt country close by to come back from, so recovery may be quicker. And where nearly everything burnt, every unburnt patch counts, on your block or your neighbour’s.

1.4

How often should this country burn?

Now we come back to the big question: was this a ‘good fire’ or a ‘bad fire’ for my block?

It’s not so much whether the fire was good or bad, but whether this fire, at this severity, this long after the last one, is something your more sensitive plant species can re-establish after.

Victorian fire ecologists answer this question with two numbers for each fire division: a minimum and a maximum tolerable fire interval.2

The minimum tolerable fire interval

This is the shortest gap between fires a vegetation community can take without losing species. It is set by the plants fire kills outright, which return only from seed: the “obligate seeders”. These include most local peas, wattles, banksias and hakeas. A seeder must grow, flower, and build a seed store before the next fire. If fire returns first, there is no seed left and the species is gone from that patch.2

There are two minimums, because severity matters as much as timing:2

  • After a high-severity fire — crowns burnt, few unburnt patches — more of the existing seed bank may have been destroyed, and there are fewer surviving plants. It takes much longer for the seed bank to build back up.
  • After a low-severity, patchy fire — canopy largely intact, plenty of unburnt islands — the minimum tolerable fire interval is shorter. Sensitive species may have survived in the unburnt patches, where the seed bank and new seed were left untouched. These patches carry them through while they regrow from seed in the burnt ground.2
Note

This means an unburnt patch in the middle of your block is worth protecting well out of proportion to its size.

The maximum tolerable fire interval

This is the longest a community can go without fire before it starts to lose the species that rely on fire to grow and reproduce.2 Grasslands that go too long without fire can be choked out by their own tussocks and lose their herbs. Some species won’t germinate until smoke or heat triggers them. A long absence of fire is not automatically a good outcome.

Where these numbers come from

A vegetation type contains a hundred or more plant species, but of those there may only be ten to fifteen that are sensitive to fire coming either too soon or not soon enough. Those species, called key fire response species, are what set the minimum and the maximum. Everything else in the community can tolerate a wider range.12

You can put even more confidence in these estimates once you have confirmed which of those key fire response species grow on your block. We will come to that in Part 2.

For example, Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest is the most widespread fire division in the Grampians foothills. It recovers fast and much of its regeneration is fire-cued. It is generally healthiest when fires come no sooner than 15 years apart and no more than 45. But large parts of the Grampians National Park have now been burnt severely twice inside fifteen years, which is when the risk grows of its seeders beginning to drop out. After the 2006 Mt Lubra fire, Grampians Rice-flower took two years to first flower and Grampians Bitter-pea took three. Flowering is just the first step: from there they need to set seed, and repeat that over enough seasons to build up a large enough seed bank before the next fire.3

What happens next

Most of what was on your block before the fire is still there, even if you can’t see it yet. Plants resprout from buds under the bark or below ground, seed stored in the soil germinates in the ash, and animals move back in as shelter and food return.

2.1

The recovery timeline

Vegetation growth after a fire progresses in recognisable stages. Fire ecologists call these growth stages, and count them from the last fire. Two patches of the same vegetation type, both in good condition, can look nothing alike, and the usual reason is how long since each last burnt. No stage is the “good” one. Different plants and animals prefer different growth stages, so the highest biodiversity generally comes with a mosaic of growth stages.

Wildlife in the first few years

In the first six months you may see more wildlife than usual. That is exposure, not recovery — the survivors have nowhere to hide. Numbers usually fall from here as food and shelter run out and predators find them easy — predation pressure measurably rises once cover is gone. This is when pest control does the most good.1053

Small mammals mostly come back from animals that survived the fire on the spot, in unburnt patches, logs, rock piles and burrows, rather than from animals moving in from outside.8 That is one more reason the unburnt patches on your block matter. Expect numbers to stay well down for the first few years and climb as the shrub layer thickens; some species peak around ten years after fire, and drought can hold the climb back.9

After a high-severity fire, bird diversity can stay low for three years or more, so a quiet block in the second year is normal — and in the Grampians heathy woodlands, patchy burns held more species than wildfire did.11

Why a mix matters

Different animals need different stages at the same time. Young regrowth is dense at ground level, good for small mammals and low-feeding birds. Mature bush has structural variety and accumulated logs. Old bush is where most hollows are. Old vegetation is now managed as an asset in its own right on public land, precisely because it is so hard to replace.3

The state’s benchmarks for public land give a sense of balance: roughly 5% juvenile, 10% adolescent, 30% mature and 20% old, with no more than 30% of fire-sensitive types below their minimum interval. These are landscape-wide targets, not property targets.36

What it means for your block

If everything around you burnt and a corner of yours didn’t, that corner may hold the only deep litter, dense shelter and standing midstorey for some distance, and may be carrying animals with nowhere else to go. In the lookup tool it shows up in section 2.1 as mature or old ground beside juvenile, as on the example block, and Your block in the landscape at the end of section 1.3 shows how much unburnt bush there is around you. When fires have come back before the minimum tolerable interval was reached, protecting the unburnt areas you already have is generally the most valuable thing you can do.

The reverse also holds: surrounded by long-unburnt country, your young regrowth may be the scarce stage, and protecting the rare species that pop up after the fire from weeds and grazing may be the most valuable thing you can do.

A note

Growth stage is counted from the last fire, which, as we discussed in 1.3, may not show unburnt patches within the fire boundary. So a patch can read as juvenile while still holding some old trees that survived. A burnt paddock tree with hollows intact is old-growth habitat whatever the age class says. Mapping your good hollow trees is a very helpful activity, covered later in the guide (section 4.5).

2.2

How plants come back — and which ones won’t

In the first weeks the bush looks dead. Most of it isn’t. Nearly every plant on your block uses one of four strategies, and knowing which is which tells you what to expect — and which species to worry about.

1. Resprouters — back in weeks

Growing points protected under thick bark or underground. Eucalypts shoot straight down the trunk and along the limbs; grasses and bracken come from underground; grass trees have been recorded shooting within a fortnight.72

Look for: shoots emerging from bark, fresh growth at the base of a trunk, green spears in a burnt tussock. If you see this, the plant is fine.

2. Obligate seeders — killed, and starting over

Killed outright, returning only from seed in the soil or released from woody fruit by heat. Most local banksias, hakeas, wattles and peas.1432

Look for: nothing at first, then a carpet of seedlings, usually late in the first year. The parent plants stay dead — cutting them back achieves nothing.

3. Fire-cued germinators — things you have never seen

Seed that sits in the soil for decades until smoke or heat breaks dormancy. It is why people find orchids, lilies and peas they are certain were never there — the orchid response after Black Saturday was documented in detail for exactly this reason.15 After the 2006 Mt Lubra fire, monitoring turned up entirely new populations of threatened plants in country unburnt for fifty or sixty years.3

Look for: anything unfamiliar, especially in the first spring. Much of it won’t be visible again for decades.

4. The space-takers

Plants built to exploit bare ground, full sun and no competition. Among natives these are the fire ephemerals: fast, short-lived, and useful for holding soil and feeding insects and seed-eaters.2 The same opening is what weeds are built for — see Part 4.

Why the seeders are the watch list

A resprouter has to be killed outright to be lost, which takes an extraordinary fire. A seeder only has to be caught at the wrong moment.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A seedling must survive, grow, flower, then keep flowering for several more seasons before there is a seed store worth anything in the ground. After the Mt Lubra fire, Grampians Rice-flower took two years to flower for the first time and Grampians Bitter-pea three — and flowering is the start, not the end.3

A mass germination is no guarantee either. Monitoring of the Mt Cassell Grevillea counted 294 seedlings in 2024 and 214 a year later — 73% survival.3 A reasonable result, and a reminder that the seedlings you see now are not the plants you will have in five years.

Burn that ground again before the seeders have rebuilt their seedbank and they are gone from the patch. That is the minimum interval in 1.4, in practice — and it is why comparing your fire history against your seeder list is the single most useful thing you can do with both. On the example block, the gap between the last two fires was 19 years. Grampians Rice-flower, which has records in the surrounding country, needed two of those to reach first flower and several more to build a seedbank worth anything. It made it — but a fire in, say, 2015 would have caught it partway.

What to watch for

  • A shrub layer that stays thin. An area that used to carry dense shrubs still open in year two or three, while the rest fills in. Compare it against less severely burnt ground.
  • A species on your seeder list you cannot find. Particularly if your place has burnt twice in twenty years.
  • Seedlings that appear then vanish. Browsed-off germination is the same outcome as no germination. After a fire there is little else for deer, rabbits and kangaroos to eat (4.4).

Part 3

Tracking the recovery

Is your block recovering the way it should? Every section here carries a protocol you can follow.

Before you decide what to do, it helps to know what you’ve got. This part covers the monitoring that tells you what survived and what is coming back: photo points, a species list, a habitat structure survey, birds, and the animals that are only out at night. Each section has a protocol you can follow, and none of them need specialist training. The aim is not a scientific study but a record you can compare year to year, so you can see whether your bush is recovering and whether the work you are doing is helping.

3.1

Setting up to watch

Monitoring only works if you can compare. A wander with a camera tells you little; the same spot, photographed the same way, three years running, tells you a great deal. Set the fixed points up first — everything else in Part 3 hangs off them.

Protocol — photo points
Where
Three to five spots covering the different parts of your block — badly burnt, lightly burnt, and anything that escaped. If your place hasn’t burnt, set them up in your best country first (6.2).
Setting up
Mark the standing position with a star picket or post. Choose a fixed landmark to frame on — a tree, a ridge line, a gate — so the shot is repeatable without measuring.
Each visit
Stand in the same place, face the same way, take the same shot. Record the point name and the date.
How often
Monthly for the first year, then seasonally. The first year is when the change is fastest and most worth having.

Monitoring plots

Pick two areas of about two hectares in the parts of the block you most want to know about, and mark the corners so you can find them again. Use the same two plots for the hollow transect5 (4.6), habitat structure (3.3) and bird surveys (3.5). Two plots lets you compare, which is what makes any of the numbers mean something.

Set the important ones up before a fire

You can add photo points at any time, and after a fire you certainly will. But the before photograph is the one you cannot go back and take, and it is worth most at the places that matter most — the patches you identified as your best country (2.2).

A point set up afterwards tells you how that ground recovered. A point set up beforehand tells you what it recovered to, and whether it got back there.

Keep a simple record

Date, what you did, what you saw. A notebook is enough. What matters is that it is continuous — gaps are what make monitoring data hard to use later.

3.2

Recording what species you have

A species list for your block is the most valuable record you can hold, and the best time to make one is before a fire. Afterwards, it is the only way to answer the question that matters: not what came back, but what didn’t.

iNaturalist makes this possible without botany. You photograph things; the app’s AI suggests an identification and a community of identifiers confirms or corrects it. Records flow through to the Atlas of Living Australia, so the list becomes part of the public record rather than sitting on your phone.

Protocol — building a species list
The method
Walk your block and photograph one of every different thing you see. Don’t try to identify anything on the spot.
Photographing so it can be identified

Before a fire

After a fire

After a fire, photograph the evidence as well as the species: epicormic shoots running down a burnt trunk, fresh growth at the base of a stump, green spears in a blackened tussock4, a carpet of seedlings. These tell you which strategy each plant is using (2.2), and therefore whether it is coming back at all.

3.3

Habitat structure survey

This survey measures the shape of the bush rather than its species — how much canopy, midstorey and ground cover there is, and how much of it is native. It is the quickest way to see which layer is lagging, and it needs no plant identification beyond native or not.

Protocol — habitat structure4
Canopy
Number of trees; species present; proportion native; how many large and how many small; hollows visible; mistletoe present; anything flowering; overall canopy cover.
Midstorey
Number of plants; number of species; proportion native; anything flowering; which natives and which invasives.
Understorey
Proportion of the ground that is bare soil, leaf litter, grasses and sedges, and broadleaf plants.
Repeat
Same plots, same season, once a year.

Run it in both monitoring plots (3.1) and compare. A canopy recovering while the midstorey stays open is the pattern to watch for — it usually means either obligate seeders lost (2.2) or browsing pressure (4.4).

Plot size and shape for this survey, and whether the proportions are estimated in bands or as numbers. The field schema gives every variable recorded but not the geometry or the estimation method.

3.4

Why birds?

Birds are the most practical thing to monitor on a recovering block, for four reasons.

  • You can detect them. They are abundant, diverse and mostly identifiable by sound, which means you can survey them without catching or disturbing anything.
  • They read the habitat for you. Different species need different layers — ground, midstorey, canopy, hollows. A bird list is effectively a readout of the structure in 3.3. Losing the midstorey birds tells you the midstorey hasn’t come back, whatever the canopy looks like.
  • They recover on a known timetable. Diversity stays low for around three years after a severe fire (2.1), so a block that is still quiet in year two is normal — and one still quiet in year five is telling you something.
  • People notice them. The first return of a familiar call is the most convincing evidence of recovery most landholders will ever get.

There are two ways to survey them, and they answer different questions: a standard survey done by ear (3.5), and a listening station that runs continuously (3.6).

3.5

Birds the traditional way

The BirdLife Australia two-hectare, twenty-minute survey is the standard method in this country. Its value is comparability: your results sit alongside everyone else’s, in the same format, going back decades.

Protocol — two-hectare, twenty-minute survey20
Where and when
A fixed two-hectare plot (3.1). Early morning is best. Avoid wind and rain.
The survey
Twenty minutes, staying within the plot. Record every species seen or heard.
For each species
Number of individuals; what it was doing; any breeding activity — nest, carrying material, feeding young.
Also record
Start time, observers, and whether there is water in the plot. Water makes a large difference to what turns up and is needed to interpret the results.
How often
Once per season, four times a year, in the same plots.

If you can’t identify birds by call, join a round rather than starting cold. Local Landcare groups and birding networks run surveys and are generally glad of company — it is the fastest way to learn, and you end up with the data either way.

3.6

Birds the automated way

A listening station is a small weatherproof-cased unit with microphones that records continuously and identifies calls automatically — three-second slices of song turned into a spectrogram, then matched by image recognition. Results appear on a phone app, usually within minutes.217 It runs unattended for months, which is the whole point — see 3.7 for what it can and can’t tell you.

What you need

3.7

What a listening station can and can’t tell you

Three things are worth knowing before you buy one, and none of them are in the manual.

1. It hears its own patch, not your property

We tested this against an experienced observer surveying a plot while we checked what the nearest station logged in the same twenty minutes. Standing in the plot, the station picked up roughly one of the birds the observer recorded. Several hundred metres away it picked up essentially none of them, and logged a different set of birds instead.

So a station’s list belongs to the ground it stands on. One unit does not cover a property, and if there is unburnt bush within earshot, some of what it hears is coming from there.

2. It will be offline more than you expect

Across our nine stations the network ran less than half the time, and by late winter seven of the nine were down — flat batteries, dropped coverage, water where it shouldn’t be. Check yours weekly. And read a quiet month as a flat battery before you read it as quiet bush.

3. It will name the odd wrong bird

Identification is done by an AI model listening for call patterns. It is right most of the time. One of our stations, beside a farm dam a long way inland, confidently reported a seabird. Treat an unusual record as a question — the recording is saved, so you or a local birder can check it.

What it is good at, and what it can’t replace

A station listens every day, at dawn, dusk and two in the morning, for months. Over the year ours picked up 117 species across the nine properties; expert observers surveying the same properties seasonally recorded 59. Night-callers, cryptic species and the bird that turns up once in June are the station’s territory.

But some birds barely vocalise. Our observers recorded a goshawk, a kite, a falcon and a firetail that no station ever picked up, and one parrot recorded by observers at five properties was never detected acoustically. Do both if you can. If you can only do one, the station is the one you can afford to run forever.

Part 4

Helping it along

The things worth doing, in the order they are worth doing them: deal with the threats first, add habitat only where it is genuinely missing, and plant last.

Once you know what country you have and what is coming back by itself, you can decide where your effort is best spent. Weeds and pest animals come first, because controlling them is what the research most consistently finds makes a difference after fire, and the window to act is short. Hollows and nest boxes come next, but only where they are genuinely missing. Thinning and replanting come last: they are the most expensive, the hardest to undo, and often not needed at all.

4.1

Why invasives are the priority

If you do one thing for wildlife after a fire, control the weeds and pest animals. That is the consistent finding from research following Black Saturday and Black Summer: controlling introduced predators and herbivores is generally the most valuable intervention available, ahead of anything you can plant or build.

What happens to predators

Foxes and cats move into burnt country almost immediately — within hours of the fire passing. Research on fire-affected Victorian landscapes found that where understorey cover was lost, pest predator activity increased around five-fold, and fox predation on medium-sized native mammals doubled.

The reason is simple. The animals that survived the fire have nowhere left to hide, and they are concentrated in whatever cover remains. Long-term small mammal trapping in Victorian parks shows the same pattern from the other side: a sharp drop in native small mammals after fire, and a slow recovery.

What happens to herbivores

Deer, rabbits and house mice arrive fast and eat the recovery. House mice are typically the first mammal back, breeding up on seed and fresh germination, and can dominate for years before native species return.9 Deer and rabbits hit the regrowth at exactly the stage it can least afford it — a browsed-off seedling is the same outcome as no germination (2.2).

Not recommended: supplementary feeding

Putting out food for surviving wildlife is well meant and generally advised against. It concentrates animals where predators can find them, supports pest species as readily as native ones, and carries disease and dependency risks. The evidence supports controlling invasives instead.

The order that follows from this runs through the rest of Part 4: work out what you have (4.2), map and prioritise it (4.3), and deal with the animals (4.4).

4.2

Weeds after fire — what to look for and where

Fire clears the ground, opens the canopy and puts nutrients in the ash bed. That is a regeneration opportunity for natives and an equally good one for weeds — and weeds are usually faster.

What makes a weed win after fire

  • Fire-cued germination — heat or smoke triggers it, so the fire itself starts the infestation.
  • A persistent seedbank — seed that has been waiting, sometimes for years.
  • Speed — establishing and setting seed before natives have closed the gap.

Where to look

Weeds arrive along edges and disturbance. Check, in roughly this order: access tracks and roadsides; watercourses and drainage lines, where rain carries seed downhill and eventually into waterways; animal pads and fence lines; and anywhere machinery worked during or after the fire, including containment lines and dozer tracks.

What this project found

Across the nine demonstration properties, the species that turned up were:

  • Woody: sallow wattle, blackberry
  • Grasses: perennial veldt grass
  • Pioneers: stinkwort, horehound, thistles
  • Bulbs, corms and tubers: cape tulip, South African weed orchid, arum lily, bridal creeper
  • New arrivals: red ink weed, which appeared on one property only after the fires

That last line matters most. A plant showing up on your place for the first time after a fire is the highest-value target you will get13 — small, beatable, and about to stop being either (4.3).

The two-year rule

Keep checking disturbed ground for at least twenty-four months. Many post-fire weeds arrive in the second year, once the ash bed has settled. The people who get caught are the ones who looked in the first spring, saw little, and stopped.

Not bringing more in

After a fire there is more traffic on your place than ever — contractors, fencing crews, gravel, hay, machinery — and nearly all of it is a weed vector. Wash down vehicles, machinery, boots and tools on the way in and out, and use certified weed-free seed, mulch, soil and gravel.

Control: match the method to the plant

What works depends on how the plant stores its energy and moves its seed. Broadly:

  • Woody legumes (sallow wattle, blackberry) — fire-triggered seedbanks mean mass germination. Plan for repeat visits across years, not one campaign.
  • Perennial grasses (veldt grass) — hardest to see, hardest to kill, easiest to spread on machinery.
  • Pioneers (stinkwort, horehound, thistles) — fast on bare ground, but the most tractable group if caught before seed set.
  • Bulbs, corms and tubers (cape tulip, weed orchid, arum lily, bridal creeper) — the ones that defeat people, because the plant you can see is not where the energy is. The AABR guide to weeds with underground reproductive structures covers these in detail.

Case study · Phill and Joan Bennett, Pomonal

Why the life cycle decides the method

The Bennetts have about twelve hectares at Church Road, half of it under a Trust for Nature covenant. Blackberry is now eradicated; thistles, bridal creeper and arum lily have been worked back. Red ink weed appeared only after the fires. Their two hardest weeds show why biology matters more than technique.

One-leaf cape tulip grows from a corm that can sit 150 mm down and produces one to three new corms a year. About half stay dormant each season and don’t come up at all. One plant sets over a thousand seeds, viable around two and a half years, and takes two to three years to reach flowering age.

So: one pass can never work, because half the population wasn’t above ground. Commit to a multi-year cycle, and get them before seed set every year without fail.

South African weed orchid has tubers only about 5 cm down, so digging genuinely works. It sprouts around June. But it self-pollinates and releases hundreds of thousands of dust-fine airborne seeds from late November, viable seven years or more.

So: the whole game is getting them out before late November — and your neighbours matter, because that seed travels.

Anything dug up is burnt, or sealed in a black plastic bag and left in the sun: a corm left lying will keep ripening. Where ground is too rocky to dig or numbers too great, they spot spray. Where they can’t reach everything, they knock the flower heads off before seed develops — not a cure, but it buys a year.

4.4

Pest animals

Fire changes the balance between native animals and pest species, and it changes it quickly. It also opens a short window where control is unusually effective.

Foxes and cats

They arrive within hours and hunt in the open. There is no realistic way for one landholder to hold a fox population down alone — foxes range widely and the gap fills. This is the clearest case for coordinated work: baiting and trapping programs run across whole landscapes, and joining one does more than anything on your own boundary.

Deer

Fallow and red deer are the main problem in this region. They damage regrowth at the stage it can least afford it, compete with native herbivores, spread weed seed and trample sensitive ground. Control is through ground and aerial shooting and trapping, and again works best coordinated — there is a regional deer program you can join rather than starting from scratch.

Rabbits

Rabbits are the one pest where fire genuinely helps you. Burnt ground makes warrens visible for the first time in years, and with little feed about, remaining rabbits take bait far more readily. If you are going to do rabbit work, the eighteen months after a fire is the moment.

Two cautions. Surviving native animals are also short of food and shelter, so plan control with that in mind. And machinery work around warrens can damage waterways and Aboriginal cultural heritage — check before you start.

Rabbits breed faster than you think

A doe can produce around 28 young a year, in litters of four to six, and is capable of re-mating within hours of giving birth. Partial control is not control — a warren left half-ripped will be back to capacity in a season.

Know your obligations

Baiting, shooting and earthworks all carry legal requirements, and some need permits. Agriculture Victoria, DEECA, your CMA, Aboriginal Victoria and your local council can tell you what applies. Ask before you start rather than after.

Use your monitoring

Trail cameras (3.9) tell you which pest species are actually present, where they move and when — which is the difference between control aimed at a problem and control aimed at a guess.

4.5

Hollows

More than 300 Australian vertebrate species depend on tree hollows — around 114 birds, 83 mammals, 79 reptiles and 27 amphibians.24 They use them for nesting, breeding, shelter, and escape from predators and weather.

Hollows cannot be hurried

Hollows form slowly, through fire scars, branch loss, fungal decay, insect activity and plain weathering. Typical formation times:

Approximate time for hollows to form, and the animals that use each size.

A hollow-bearing tree lost today cannot be replaced in a human lifetime. That single fact drives most of what follows.

What fire does

Fire both makes and destroys hollows. Scars begin the decay that eventually forms one, and historically patchy, low-severity fire was part of how hollows came about. But severe fire collapses hollow trunks, brings down limbs and kills ancient trees outright — and it does that far faster than new hollows appear.

After a severe fire, hollow availability often drops, at least for a while, and competition for what remains rises. But a burnt tree is not necessarily an empty one. Fire can open new hollows in scarred trunks and limbs, and trees killed by fire can go on forming hollows for decades as they decay. The trees on your place that carry hollows now, burnt or unburnt, are doing work for a wide area.

What to do

  • Keep standing dead trees. Stags are often the best hollow trees on a property and are the first thing people tidy up. Where they can safely be kept, keep them (see 0.1 on hazard trees).
  • Keep old paddock trees. A single large old tree in the open may hold more hollows than a hectare of regrowth.
  • Leave fallen timber where it lies. Logs are ground-level habitat, and after fire there is very little other cover.
  • Only then consider adding hollows — and work out first whether you need to (4.6).

4.7

Boxes, carved hollows and pods

If 4.6 says your block needs more hollows, there are three ways to add them, and they suit different animals.

Nest boxes

The demonstration allocation used in this project was nine boxes per property across three designs — a squirrel glider box, a microbat box, and a small glider or antechinus box — built by the Stawell Men’s Shed. Eighty-one went up across the nine sites.

Three designs rather than one is deliberate. You rarely know what is looking for a hollow until something moves in, and occupancy across different designs is itself the answer (4.6).

Installing and checking boxes
Where
In the part of your block with the fewest natural hollows (4.6). Spread them rather than clustering.
Mounting
Hang on wire run through poly pipe, or fix with bugle batten screws and a washer. Grease the thread. Whatever you use, allow for the tree growing.

Carved hollows

A hollow can be cut directly into a living tree by a climbing arborist, producing something closer to the real thing than a box: better insulated, longer lasting, and part of the tree rather than bolted to it. It needs a specialist, so it suits a few high-value trees rather than a whole property. Log-box hollows — assembled from a hollowed section of log — are a middle option that a group can build in a workshop.

Habitat pods and ground habitat

Not everything nests in trees. Ground-dwelling mammals and reptiles lose their cover entirely in a severe fire, and ground-level structures help while the understorey rebuilds: habitat pods, tile grids, and basking tiles for reptiles. Trust for Nature supplied eight pods per site to the three Little Desert properties in this project.

4.9

Do I need to replant?

The most common question after a fire, and the answer is nearly always not yet.

Planting into regenerating ground is one of the few genuinely counterproductive things you can do. The seedlings you can’t see yet are competing with what you put in, tubestock brings its own weed seed and genetics, and the money is gone at the point where weed and browsing control would buy you far more.

Decision — do I need to replant?
1 — Wait a full growing season, ideally two
The first spring after the fire is when most of what will return appears, and some of it appears only then (section 2.2).
2 — Record what comes up
Walk the block and photograph one of everything (section 3.2). You are building a list, not identifying on the spot.
3 — Compare against what should be there
Your pre-1750 vegetation type (section 1.2), or any species list recorded before the fire.
4 — Ask what is actually missing
Not “does it look bare”, but: is a whole layer absent two seasons on, or a species you know was there?
5 — Then plant, into the gap
Targeted, not spread across the paddock.

Good reasons to plant

  • A species burnt out locally — most likely where two fires fell inside the minimum interval (section 1.4) and a seeder never returned.
  • A structural layer still absent after two seasons, shrubs especially.
  • Connectivity — linking two patches so animals can move between them.
  • Erosion — actively moving soil. Urgent, and properly soil stabilisation rather than revegetation.

Reasons that aren’t

  • It looks bare. It will, for a while.
  • You want to be doing something. There is plenty to do — see below.
  • There is funding for plants. Funding is not a diagnosis.

If you do plant

Match the vegetation type — your EVC benchmark (section 1.2) lists what belongs. Use local provenance stock, and weed-free mulch, soil and gravel (section 4.3). Plant into gaps rather than across regenerating ground, and guard against browsing, or you are running a feeding station for deer and rabbits.

While you wait

Protect what survived, control the weeds coming through the ash bed, deal with browsing pressure, and set up the monitoring that will tell you in two years whether anything is missing. Part 5 puts that in order.

Part 6

If you haven’t burnt yet

Three things worth doing while the bush is intact. None of them take long, and every one is worth more now than it will be afterwards.

If your bush hasn’t burnt, or hasn’t burnt recently, now is the best time to prepare. Much of what this guide asks you to do after a fire is easier and more useful if you have a record of what was there before. Starting a species list, setting up photo points and doing the Part 1 desk work now means that if a fire does come, you can judge the recovery against what you had rather than guessing.

6.1

Start a species list

The most valuable record you can hold is a list of what grows on your place. Start it now, on iNaturalist, and build it across the seasons.

You don’t need to identify anything. That is the part that stops people before they begin. Walk your block, and whenever you see something in flower that you don’t recognise, photograph it — whole plant, leaves, and the flower — and post it. The app suggests a name and other people confirm or correct it. Your job is to capture an example, not to know what it is.

Walk it seasonally. Most plants are only identifiable while they are flowering, and they don’t all flower at once.1816 A walk each season for a couple of years turns up things a single spring walk never will. Section 3.2 has the detail on photographing plants so they can actually be identified.

What it gives you later

Zoom to your property on iNaturalist and you have every species anyone has recorded there. Print it off and it becomes a checklist: after a fire, walk the block and tick off what comes back.

What is still unticked after two or three seasons answers the question that matters — not what returned, but what didn’t. That is what decides whether replanting is warranted (4.9), and it is not a question you can answer from memory.

It works the other way too. Anything you find that is not on your list is new, and after a fire a new plant is either a fire-cued germinator that has been waiting in the soil for decades2 (2.2) or a weed worth acting on early (4.2).

For a head start on what to look for, the Atlas of Living Australia will give you a checklist of everything recorded in the country around you — 2.3 shows what one looks like. It is a prompt rather than a record of your block.

6.2

Photo points in your best country

Photo points are the cheapest monitoring there is, and the before photograph is the one you cannot go back and take.

Put them in your best country first. The richest understorey, the densest midstorey, the stand of old trees with hollows. These are the places you most want to be able to protect, and the places where knowing what was lost will matter most.

You can add more points after a fire, and you will — across badly burnt, lightly burnt and unburnt ground (3.1). But those tell you how the ground recovered. A point set up beforehand tells you what it recovered to, and whether it got back there.

Section 3.1 has the protocol: mark the standing position, frame on a fixed landmark, record the point name and date.5 Three to five points and an afternoon.

Map the good patches while you are at it

Mark on an aerial photo where the best understorey is, where the midstorey is densest, where the old trees stand. You are recording two things: what to compare against afterwards, and what to defend first if you ever get the choice. The habitat structure survey (3.3) gives you a proper way to record those layers, and doing it once now is worth more than three times later.

6.3

Do the desk work now

Part 1 is a set of lookups that take an afternoon and do not require a fire to be useful.2 Doing them in advance means that if one comes, you already know what you are dealing with.

  • Your vegetation type (1.2) — the EVC and division your block sits in, and the benchmark list of what belongs there.
  • Your fire history (1.3) — every fire on the record, and the gaps between them.
  • Your tolerable fire intervals (1.4) — the minimum and maximum your vegetation can take.
  • Your growth stage (1.5) — how long since the last fire, and what that means for what is there now.

Then sort your species list

Take the list from 6.1 and put every species into a fire-response group — resprouter, seeder, or fire-cued (2.2 has the method). The seeders are your watch list.417

Now put that against your fire history. If the gap since your last fire is shorter than the minimum interval for your vegetation, the seeders on your list are the species at risk, and you know which ones to look for before anything happens rather than afterwards.

The short version

Start a species list. Put photo points in your best country. Look up your vegetation type, fire history and intervals. That is a weekend of work, and after a fire it is the difference between measuring your recovery and guessing at it.

7.1

Who can help

Very little of this is best done alone, and most of it is cheaper and easier with help.

  • Your local Landcare group or network. The first call. They run surveys you can join, often hold shared equipment such as thermal scopes, and know your district’s weeds and who else is working on them.
  • Your Catchment Management Authority. Can pull your property’s fire history and mapping (1.3), and administers much of the funding.
  • DEECA for vegetation, fire ecology and threatened species questions; Agriculture Victoria for weed and pest declarations, control methods and legal obligations (4.4).
  • Trust for Nature for conservation covenants and, in this project, habitat pods.
  • Your local Men’s Shed for nest boxes. This worked well here and is worth asking about.
  • Local ecologists and birders. An expert survey once or twice a year complements anything automated (3.7), and local birders will verify uncertain records.

Glossary

TermWhat it means
BioregionA large area sharing geology, landform, climate and vegetation. Four cover this region (1.1).
EVCEcological Vegetation Class. The mapped vegetation type for a piece of ground, with a number and a name (1.2).
EVDEcological Vegetation Division. EVCs grouped by how they behave; the level that carries the fire intervals (1.4).
Tolerable fire intervalThe shortest and longest gaps between fires a vegetation type can take without losing species (1.4).
Growth stageHow long since the last fire: juvenile, young, mature or old (1.5).
ResprouterA plant that survives fire and reshoots from protected buds, in bark or underground (2.2).
Obligate seederA plant killed by fire that returns only from seed. The group most at risk from repeat fire (2.2).
Epicormic shootsNew growth emerging straight from bark on a trunk or limb — the clearest sign a tree survived.
Fire ephemeralA short-lived plant that germinates after fire, holds the soil and then largely disappears (2.2).
SeedbankSeed held in the soil or in woody fruit, waiting for the conditions — often heat or smoke — to germinate.
DBHDiameter at breast height. The standard way to record tree size (4.6).
Hollow-bearing treeA tree with cavities animals can use. Living or dead (4.5).
StagA standing dead tree. Often the best hollow habitat on a property (4.5).
ProvenanceWhere planting stock originally came from. Local provenance is adapted to local conditions (4.9).
Listening stationAn automated unit that records and identifies bird calls continuously (3.6).
