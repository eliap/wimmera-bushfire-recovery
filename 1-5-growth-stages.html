1.5

Growth stages

Two patches of the same vegetation type, both in good condition, can look nothing alike. The usual reason is how long since each last burnt. Fire ecologists track this as growth stage, and the key point is that no stage is the good one.

Years since fire for each growth stage, in the region’s common vegetation divisions.1
Vegetation divisionJuvenileYoungMatureOld
Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest0–33–1010–3535+
Forby Forest0–33–1010–150150+
Inland Plains Woodland0–44–1919–120120+
Treed Swampy Wetland0–33–88–3030+
Basalt Grassland0–22–44–66+

Note how different the rows are. Bush that burnt thirty-five years ago is old in Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest and barely middle-aged in Forby Forest, while Basalt Grassland is old just six years after a fire.

Inland Plains Woodland rarely carries fire at all. Its stages describe what follows the unusual fire that does get through, and its 150-year maximum is only a best estimate. Some stands can be degraded by a single fire.

In the lookup tool: section 1.5

In the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool, section 1.5, Growth stages, works out the stage of each part of your block from the years since it last burnt, using this table. While the section is on screen, the map colours each part of the block by its stage: light green for juvenile, mid green for young, teal for mature, dark blue-green for old. Hover over a stage bar to highlight that part.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

Both vegetation types on the example block last burnt in December 2024, so both are juvenile: two years into a stage that runs to three. Before that fire, 18 years after 2006, the Heathy Woodland was mature (10–35 years in its division) and the Damp Sands Herb-rich Woodland was early in Forby Forest’s long mature stage. The 2024 fire took the whole block back to the youngest stage, with whatever escaped it carrying everything older.

Why a mix matters

Different animals need different stages at the same time. Young regrowth is dense at ground level, good for small mammals and low-feeding birds. Mature bush has structural variety and accumulated logs. Old bush is where most hollows are, and hollows cannot be rushed (section 4.5). Old vegetation is now managed as an asset in its own right on public land, precisely because it is so hard to replace.2

The state’s benchmarks for public land give a sense of balance: roughly 5% juvenile, 10% adolescent, 30% mature and 20% old, with no more than 30% of fire-sensitive types below their minimum interval. These are landscape targets, not property targets.23

What it means for your block

After a large fire your block may be scarce in a specific way. If everything around you burnt and a corner of yours didn’t, that corner may hold the only deep litter, dense shelter and standing midstorey for some distance, and may be carrying animals with nowhere else to go. The lookup tool points this out when part of your block escaped the latest fire. Hollows are a separate story: fire destroys some and opens up others, so burnt country is not automatically empty of them (section 4.5). Either way, protecting what you already have comes before adding anything new (section 5.1).

The reverse also holds: surrounded by long-unburnt country, your young regrowth is the scarce stage.

One caution

Growth stage is counted from the last fire, so a patch can read as juvenile while still holding old trees that survived. A burnt paddock tree with hollows intact is old-growth habitat whatever the age class says.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

What Part 1 told us about the example block

The readings pointed at one question above all: do the obligate seeders come back? At 18 years the shrubs should have set enough seed to survive the 2024 fire, but the margin was thin enough to be worth checking rather than assuming. That meant watching the shrub layer through the second and third year (section 2.2), and recording what came up in the first spring (section 3.2).

The unburnt ground mattered more than usual too. With the whole block reset to juvenile, any patch that did not burn holds the only mature understorey and midstorey on the place. Hollows were a separate question, because they sit in the old trees, burnt or not, and fire both destroys and opens them (section 4.5).

None of this took long. The lookup tool does the lookups in a couple of minutes. By hand, with NatureKit and the fire history maps, it is an afternoon.

What happened next

The monitoring bore the reading out. The listening station on this property recorded around 70 bird species over the year and was one of the two most reliable in the network; thermal surveys found it the most active of the nine sites at night, sugar gliders included. Hollows were the thin part — six hollow-bearing trees in one survey plot and three in the other, which is what put nest boxes on the list here (4.6).4

Needed

The Inland Plains Woodland, Treed Swampy Wetland and Basalt Grassland rows fold Cheal’s five stages for each into the guide’s four (renewal and juvenility together as Juvenile, the last stage as Old). Worth an ecologist’s check.

Which term throughout — “young” or “adolescent”? Cheal’s formal stages for Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest run renewal, juvenility, adolescence, maturity, waning, senescence; Forby Forest has vigorous maturity and stasis instead of maturity and waning, so the two divisions do not even share a vocabulary. Recommendation: keep the plain set in the table and gloss the formal terms once in the glossary. The percentages above are attributed to La Trobe University work — that publication still needs pinning down.
