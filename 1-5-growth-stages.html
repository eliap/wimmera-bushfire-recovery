1.5

Growth stages

Two patches of the same vegetation type, both in good condition, can look nothing alike. The usual reason is how long since each last burnt. Fire ecologists track this as growth stage. No stage is the “good” one. Different plants and animals prefer different growth stages, so the highest biodiversity generally comes with a mosaic of growth stages.

Years since fire for each growth stage, in the region’s common vegetation divisions.1
Vegetation divisionJuvenileYoungMatureOld
Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest0–33–1010–3535+
Forby Forest0–33–1010–150150+
Inland Plains Woodland0–44–1919–120120+
Treed Swampy Wetland0–33–88–3030+
Basalt Grassland0–22–44–66+

Note how different the rows are. Bush that burnt thirty-five years ago is old in Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest and barely middle-aged in Forby Forest, while Basalt Grassland is old just six years after a fire.

Inland Plains Woodland rarely carries fire at all. Its stages describe what follows the unusual fire that does get through, and its 150-year maximum is only a best estimate. Some stands can be degraded by a single fire.

In the lookup tool: section 1.5

In the Wimmera Bushfire Ecology Lookup Tool, section 1.5, Growth stages, works out the stage of each part of your block from the years since it last burnt, using this table. While the section is on screen, the map colours each part of the block by its stage, from pale green for juvenile to dark green for old. Click a row to highlight that part of the block.

Follow along · the example block near Pomonal

The 67 hectares of Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest that burnt in 2025 is juvenile, early in a stage that runs to three years. The 22 hectares that escaped last burnt in 2006, twenty years ago, so it is mature (10–35 years in its division), and the Damp Sands Herb-rich Woodland is early in Forby Forest’s long mature stage. The block now carries two stages side by side, and the older one is the scarcer: about four-fifths of the bush within 5 km burnt in 2025 (section 1.3), so the block’s unburnt bush is one of the patches the surrounding country will recover from.

Why a mix matters

Different animals need different stages at the same time. Young regrowth is dense at ground level, good for small mammals and low-feeding birds. Mature bush has structural variety and accumulated logs. Old bush is where most hollows are. Old vegetation is now managed as an asset in its own right on public land, precisely because it is so hard to replace.2

The state’s benchmarks for public land give a sense of balance: roughly 5% juvenile, 10% adolescent, 30% mature and 20% old, with no more than 30% of fire-sensitive types below their minimum interval. These are landscape-wide targets, not property targets.23

What it means for your block

If everything around you burnt and a corner of yours didn’t, that corner may hold the only deep litter, dense shelter and standing midstorey for some distance, and may be carrying animals with nowhere else to go. In the lookup tool it shows up in section 1.5 as mature or old ground beside juvenile, as on the example block, and Your block in the landscape at the end of section 1.3 shows how much unburnt bush there is around you. When fires have come back before the minimum tolerable interval was reached, protecting the unburnt areas you already have is generally the most valuable thing you can do.

The reverse also holds: surrounded by long-unburnt country, your young regrowth may be the scarce stage, and protecting the rare species that pop up after the fire from weeds and grazing may be the most valuable thing you can do.

A note

Growth stage is counted from the last fire, which, as we discussed in 1.3, may not show unburnt patches within the fire boundary. So a patch can read as juvenile while still holding some old trees that survived. A burnt paddock tree with hollows intact is old-growth habitat whatever the age class says. Mapping your good hollow trees is a very helpful activity, covered later in the guide (section 4.5).

Needed

The Inland Plains Woodland, Treed Swampy Wetland and Basalt Grassland rows fold Cheal’s five stages for each into the guide’s four (renewal and juvenility together as Juvenile, the last stage as Old). Worth an ecologist’s check.

Which term throughout — “young” or “adolescent”? Cheal’s formal stages for Grassy / Heathy Dry Forest run renewal, juvenility, adolescence, maturity, waning, senescence; Forby Forest has vigorous maturity and stasis instead of maturity and waning, so the two divisions do not even share a vocabulary. Recommendation: keep the plain set in the table and gloss the formal terms once in the glossary.
